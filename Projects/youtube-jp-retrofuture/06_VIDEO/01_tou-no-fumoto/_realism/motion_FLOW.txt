Live-action footage of real people in a real place, photographed on a cinema camera — not animation, not CGI, not a render. No writing, lettering or numbers anywhere in the picture except at most one shop sign carrying a single correct Japanese word. The camera is locked off on a tripod and does not move at all for the whole shot — no push, no pull, no pan, no tilt, no drift, no handheld shake, the framing identical in the first and last frame. It stands exactly where a neighbour standing in the lane across from the shop would stand, at standing eye height, and everything is seen from that one fixed place. Starting from exactly the framing of the first frame: The shopkeeper, seen from behind, pushes the heavy wooden shutter up its runners — it rattles and rises in one long shove, the slats folding away overhead — he props it, then stands in his own doorway a moment with one hand on the small of his back, looking down the lane, before he turns and goes in. Everyone moves gently and without hurry, the way people move early in the morning: each gesture slow and deliberate, the one action spread across the whole eight seconds, nothing quick, nothing sudden, nothing snapped or jerked. Only the person and the things they touch move; everything else in the frame stays where it is. The lamp filament flickers; a cloth noren in the next doorway lifts and settles in the air. Light: the cold even blue of just before sunrise, no direct sun on the ground, one street lamp still burning warm, thin morning haze in the lane softening everything beyond a few metres; muted natural colour, nothing bright, nothing burnt out. No speed ramp and no slow-motion effect. Nobody speaks, mouths stay closed, the exchange is in gesture and eyes only. Nobody looks at the camera and nobody performs for it; faces are seen in three-quarter view or looking down at what the hands are doing, never frontal and never filling the frame.
